The group studies why parasites become more or less harmful, and how the environment tips the balance.

Led by Ronaldo de Carvalho Augusto at IHPE, the team follows the human blood fluke Schistosoma mansoni through its whole life cycle, in snails, in fresh water and in mammals, to learn how temperature, pollutants and co-infecting pathogens reshape its development, virulence and transmission. It combines experimental infections, epigenomics and functional genomics with field work in Brazil and Mozambique to predict where schistosomiasis may emerge next, including in southern Europe. The group was established with support from the French National Research Agency (ANR-22-CPJ1-0056-01).

Research

Tropical diseases of today, European diseases of tomorrow

Virulence is not a fixed trait of a parasite. It emerges from the encounter between a parasite, its hosts and the water, food and microbes around them. The group asks how that encounter is changing, and what it means for disease.

The system

One parasite, two hosts, two swims through water

Schistosoma mansoni alternates between a freshwater snail, Biomphalaria glabrata, and a mammal. Between hosts, two short-lived larvae, the miracidium and the cercaria, swim freely and meet whatever the water carries.

Every transition is a developmental switch, and every switch is a point where the environment can push the parasite toward higher or lower virulence. That makes schistosomes a powerful model for environmental parasitology.

Environment and virulence

Can environmental cues change a parasite?

A schistosome reads its environment at every step. Free-living larvae swim through water whose temperature, chemistry and contaminants vary from site to site; inside its hosts, the parasite meets diet, immunity and other pathogens. The group asks how these cues reprogram its development, virulence and transmission.

Larvae are exposed to realistic stressors, from warming to pesticides, bisphenols and PFAS, and the consequences are followed through the whole life cycle, including in the next generation. Correlative imaging (SEM, TEM and confocal microscopy) of the worm surface, the tegument, reveals how these cues alter the parasite’s main interface with its host.

Molecular mechanisms

Where is virulence written?

Schistosomes change body plan several times without changing their DNA sequence. Chromatin, histone marks and non-coding RNAs carry much of that information, and they also respond to the environment.

The group maps these layers across the life cycle and during sexual maturation, and develops methods that work on the tiny amounts of material a single larval stage provides: ChIP, automated ChIPmentation and a simple ATAC-seq protocol for population epigenetics.

Disease severity

What happens when parasites share a host?

Malaria and schistosomiasis overlap across much of the tropics, and many people carry both parasites at once. This research line examines how co-infection affects host health, from survival to organ pathology, and how the two parasites influence each other inside the same host.

Field data and experimental infections are combined to follow the dynamics of parasites that share a host, including how the order and timing of infections shape their growth, reproduction and transmission.

Vector competence

Why do some snails transmit and others resist?

Transmission starts only if the miracidium is compatible with its snail. The group labels larvae with non-transgenic fluorescent dyes to follow them inside the snail, and studies snail microRNAs and clock genes linked to resistance in Biomphalaria and Bulinus.

With partners at Fiocruz, the team also tests near-infrared spectroscopy as a fast, non-invasive way to tell snail species apart and detect infection in the field.
